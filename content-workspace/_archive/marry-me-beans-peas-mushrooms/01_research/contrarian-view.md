# Contrarian View — High-Protein "Marry Me" Beans, Peas & Mushrooms

## The strongest pushback: derivative recipe posts can undercut the original creator

Food blogging runs on search traffic — ad impressions and affiliate links tied to a recipe ranking well for its name. Nora Taylor built "Marry Me Chickpeas" into a genuinely popular, well-tested recipe. Every remix post that publishes a variant (different beans, different cream, a "high-protein" spin) and ranks for a query like "marry me chickpeas high protein" is, in aggregate, traffic that could have landed on her site instead. This isn't hypothetical outrage-bait — it's the actual economics that food creators depend on, and the recipe-blog ecosystem is notorious for exactly this kind of uncredited or thinly-credited scraping and rehashing.

There's a second, quieter version of the same objection: this recipe wasn't developed in Aaron's kitchen through trial, error, and taste — it was designed in a chat window with an AI, then cooked once. That's a different kind of authorship than the iterative testing a recipe developer like Nora actually did (multiple batches, reader feedback, refinement over time). Presenting it with the same confidence as a tested recipe risks overstating how proven it is.

## Why the post holds up anyway

- **The credit is real and load-bearing, not decorative.** The post should link to Nora's original prominently — not a buried footnote, but the framing device: "this is my riff on her recipe, go make hers first." That's the honest way to have this be additive to her rather than competitive with her — readers who want the proven, tested original are one click away, and there's no attempt to out-rank "marry me chickpeas" as a search term (the title should stay specific: "beans, peas & mushrooms," not a generic variant of her title).
- **The post should be honest about what it is**: a home cook's one-off riff, developed live with an AI assistant for a specific goal (protein, no sun-dried tomatoes on hand, wife likes peas) — not a tested-and-refined recipe developer's release. Say so. Readers can calibrate expectations accordingly, and it's a more interesting story anyway (see: AI as sous-chef, not replacement for actual cooking).
- **This is consistent with how Aaron already writes about AI** — as amplification of a human doing the actual work (tasting, adjusting, deciding "peas because my wife likes them"), not replacement of the creative act. The AI proposed options; Aaron picked, cooked, and presumably tasted it. That's worth being explicit about rather than presenting the recipe as if it emerged fully formed.

Don't labor it — this is a light, fun post, not an essay on food-blog SEO ethics. One paragraph, tops.
